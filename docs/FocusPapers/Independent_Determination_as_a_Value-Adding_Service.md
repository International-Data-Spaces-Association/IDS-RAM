# Independent Determination as a Value-Adding Service in Decentralized Data Spaces

Decentralized data spaces can accommodate optional, competing value-adding services while preserving participant sovereignty. The related focus paper, [Value-Adding Services in Decentralized Data Spaces](Value-Adding_Services_in_Decentralized_Dataspaces.md), describes matchmaking and billing services. This paper describes an **Independent Determination Service**: an independently accountable service that evaluates a defined condition for a particular proposed interaction and issues a context-bound, machine-verifiable claim.

The service operates within the governance rules of the [Data Space Governance Authority (DSGA)](https://kb.internationaldataspaces.org/external/rulebook/006_DSGA) and the applicable [Dataspace Trust Framework(s) (DTFs)](https://kb.internationaldataspaces.org/external/rulebook/009_Dataspace_Trust_Frameworks). The Decentralized Claims Protocol (DCP) can be used to present the claim, while negotiation and data exchange remain governed by the Dataspace Protocol (DSP). This pattern proposes no new IDS role and requires no change to DSP or DCP.

## Independent Determination Service

An independent determination service can be offered by one or more independent service provider organizations. A participant may decide that, for a defined class of contract offers, negotiation or transfer is conditional on a current interaction-specific determination from an issuer it accepts. The service does not replace the participant's policies or make the final decision to contract or transfer. It provides a machine-verifiable claim about one proposed interaction that the participant's own control plane can consume alongside its other policy inputs.

This differs from a standing trust credential. Rather than evidencing a general status or role, the claim records that a defined rule or condition has been evaluated against the context of a particular interaction. The applicable rule can be set by the participant, by its contracts or, where a data space chooses, through the applicable governance and trust framework. Several independent issuers can coexist, and each participant remains free to decide which issuers and which claim semantics it accepts.

Before negotiation, the consumer requests a determination against a specific offer. The service evaluates the information required by the relevant rule. This can include the offer metadata and contextual facts provided by the parties, such as the parties, asset, purpose, jurisdiction or execution context. The service may determine that the condition is satisfied, not satisfied or requires referral for human determination. Only a positive determination results in a claim.

The resulting claim is bound to the specific interaction, for example by identifying the offer, parties, asset and purpose. It can also carry the applicable rule reference, a validity period and a status reference so that revocation or supersession can be checked without requiring a live call to the issuer. The claim confers no general access right and does not oblige the provider to proceed. It is a necessary input only where the provider's own policy makes it so, and all other policies continue to apply.

The claim can be presented using DCP as an additional proof during contract negotiation. The provider's control plane verifies the accepted issuer, proof, interaction binding, validity and status and records the determination reference in the agreement. Where one agreement supports several transfers, the provider can re-check status at transfer initiation for the governed class. If a required claim is absent, expired, revoked or does not match the interaction context, the governed interaction does not proceed.

The independent determination service is not a data-plane destination and does not need to receive or hold the underlying data. Contract negotiation and data exchange remain peer-to-peer between provider and consumer and are governed by DSP. The participant retains sovereignty and responsibility for final policy evaluation and enforcement.

Implementations can provide different assurance levels. A basic implementation treats the determination as a policy input to the participant's control plane. A higher-assurance implementation can bind execution technically to the presence of a current valid determination so that the governed operation cannot proceed without it. This changes the strength of enforcement, not the architectural role of the service.

The pattern introduces several governance and implementation considerations. The issuer and relying participant must share the semantics of the claim and the applicable rule; validity, status and rule versioning must address the risk of stale determinations; and the participant's policy engine must be able to match the claim's binding fields against the negotiation context. Where a fresh determination is required for every transfer, the participant is deliberately choosing an additional runtime dependency for that class of interaction.
